personal achievements are amazing because of the sheer range of possibilities they hold, say from climbing everest to building a successful enterprise. they bring excitement, celebration, and heaps of praise. posting them in a digital space gives a sense of accomplishment. and then, 
when the feeling fades sometimes slowly, sometimes all at once, you’re left wondering

is this all there is? what's the point?

take the example of getting tenure as a professor- four years of undergraduate study, two years of a master’s, five years of doctoral work, seven years as an assistant professor, all without knowing whether you’ll be selected or rejected in the end. you invest your time and effort into attaining a goal you believe will make life feel different and meaningful. when you finally get it, there’s relief. and yet, a question quietly surfaces-

is this it?

are we more than the sum of the seconds, minutes, and days of our lives?

life runs on continuity, on routines that make it predictable. seasons change. we brush our teeth every morning. this predictability gives coherence to life. personal achievement is actually an outcome of this framework. you know what you want, and you adjust your life to smoothen the process. you schedule your time, meet the right people, do the necessary work, until it’s achieved.
say if you want to lose weight, you follow a diet, exercise, and stay consistent. this framework gives life a linear structure.

purpose exists as a goal, something that answers the question of why you wake up each day. 
to achieve tenure, for instance, you need papers, grants, and recognition. that purpose generates smaller intermediate goals, helping you inch closer to the larger one.

but what is the significance of doing all this? unless you feel that you matter beyond this space and this time, your achievements, 
or even you yourself, may begin to feel insignificant.

“i think my work hasn’t achieved the quality that it should have,” says a not so famous friend of yours.

when life gives you a reason to live for others, when you want others to reach their own potential, you experience something that transcends space and time. this is relationship. this is meaning. someone once studied the life of buddha and concluded that the boatman was more content than buddha himself, because he ferried people across the river every day. he had a routine, a purpose (to cross the river), and a significance that he was helping others on their journey.

a story that is not your own, but one you are part of.

personal achievements are not meaning.
though they are easy to quantify and is quite tangible, like the house you want, the job you aim for, the salary you expect. it feels comfortable to chase things we understand.

meaningfulness does not necessarily bring happiness, but personal achievements often do.

participating in someone else’s story is a good place to begin. 
it may be uncomfortable. you might put effort into another person’s project instead of your own, and it may seem like you gain nothing in return.

yet the pursuit of meaning is beautiful and profound in itself. it means someone allowed you to participate in a story that isn’t yours. and in turn, you should allow others to generate meaning through you.

in my limited lifespan (of 45 years ;)), i can say that the personal achievements always pale in comparison to the effect you have on others.
