# The Pune Trip

The plan was simple. Go to Pune, attend a conference, present my poster, meet a few people, come back.

Naturally, things had to go wrong before the trip even began.

I had applied for the APARC conference in Pune. The registration itself was around ₹8,000, which is not exactly pocket change, but my guide had asked me to look at the dates, so I did. The conference was from 12 to 16 October. There was also an Early Career Researcher (ECR) event on the 11th. I applied for that too, and they accepted me.

Good. Poster prepared, poster printed, train booked. The train was supposed to leave the next evening. Mentally, I had already started travelling. You know that feeling when the important things are done and your brain has moved on to imagining the trip? I was there.

Then, in the morning, I got a message.

My ticket was not confirmed.

For a few seconds, I just stared at the screen. What now? The conference was happening, the poster was printed, the dates were fixed, and apparently the railway reservation system had decided that my participation was optional.

I panicked. Not the productive kind of panic where you make a list and solve things one by one. The other kind, where your brain opens seventeen tabs and none of them loads.

Then I remembered my guide saying, “Why don't you go by flight?”

At that point, this sounded less like a suggestion and more like divine intervention. I checked the prices. They had gone from around ₹4,000 to ₹10,000.

Ten thousand rupees. For a flight.

But I was in no state to think properly, so I booked it. The flight was early in the morning on the 11th, around 4:30 a.m., and would land in Pune by 6 a.m. Somehow, it never occurred to me that I could take a bus. A bus! Much cheaper, perfectly reasonable, and probably the first thing a person with a functioning brain would consider.

Anyway, the ticket was booked. Problem solved, money gone.

I had dinner at the mess, left at night, and reached the airport around midnight. I had hours to kill. I did have lounge access, but the queue was so long that I looked at it, looked at myself, and decided I was quite comfortable outside. I read books and waited for the morning to arrive. At some point, I think I had accepted that this was just how the Pune trip was going to begin.

The flight landed ahead of time. My checked-in baggage also arrived surprisingly quickly. For once, the universe seemed to be apologising.

Then came Pune airport.

I don't know who designed the cab pickup arrangement there, but the cabs seemed to be on the second or third floor, or somewhere up in the clouds. It took me about thirty minutes to book one. Three drivers cancelled. The fourth finally accepted, though he added some extra charges. I was too tired to argue.

“Okay, fine,” I thought. “Let us just reach the guesthouse.”

The driver turned out to be a friendly fellow. We talked about AI, Pune, food, agriculture, jobs, and pretty much everything in between. He even offered to get me vada pav.

At that hour.

I hadn't even brushed my teeth.

I appreciated the gesture, but I think we both knew that vada pav was not happening just yet.

I reached the guesthouse at around 7:20 a.m. IITM had arranged accommodation for us at Prithvi Hall of Residence. I was told that I had a shared room. Room 705. I went in, rested for a bit, got ready in about half an hour, and checked the venue. It was only a six-minute walk away.

Six minutes. Even I could manage that without booking a cab.

I handed over the room key in case my roommate arrived while I was out and started walking towards the venue.

Two women were walking ahead of me. They seemed to be talking about PhD-related things, so I assumed they were from the institute. When we reached the road, I stopped. I thought about crossing, moved forward, then pulled myself back. Finally, I decided to follow them. If they crossed safely, I would cross safely too. A very scientific method of road crossing, if you ask me.

At the IITM entrance, the guard asked us to sign next to our names on the list. The two women found their names. I was next.

“Name?”

“Mohit. Mohit Singh.”

One of them immediately recognised me.

It was Usha.

She had done a one-year postdoc at my college, and before that she had been at SPL. One of my friends had done his master's project there. And here we were, at the entrance of IITM, in Pune, after I had nearly lost my mind over a train ticket a few hours earlier.

The world is very small. Sometimes it waits until you are standing at a security gate to remind you.

We laughed about how I had followed them across the road. Usha joked that she had cursed me while crossing because I had first moved forward and then backed away like a confused pigeon.

The other woman was Niyati.

She wasn't feeling well and looked quite weak. This was the first time I had noticed her. I didn't know then that I would end up remembering this particular morning so clearly.

We started talking, and somehow the three of us clicked almost immediately. There was no formal getting-to-know-you phase, no awkward effort to keep a conversation alive. It felt as if our frequencies had matched from the beginning.

The conference went on. All through those days, the three of us sat together and went to lunch together. Somewhere between sessions and conversations, we became a little group.

There was just one problem. The food at Prithvi.

I'm sorry, but the breakfast was not doing it for me. The poha was unbearable, and the biryani was also pretty bad. I don't want to start a diplomatic incident with the people responsible for feeding us, but I must be honest. We ordered yoghurt and buttermilk to refresh ourselves and decided that we deserved a proper meal outside.

So, naturally, I planned an outing.

I was the planner. This is an important detail because almost nothing I planned went exactly as planned. But planning is still important. In civil engineering, there is a philosophy of building as you go. We adopted that philosophy for the evening.

We decided to explore the market around Laxmi Road. Niyati was still unwell and didn't want to walk much. At one point, she had even considered taking a cab from Prithvi to the conference venue, which was six minutes away. I had no idea how we were going to survive an entire market outing, but we went anyway.

We did darshan at a temple from outside and then went to a saree shop. Not even the saree shop I had initially selected. Plans, as I said, were mostly decorative.

We entered the shop and stayed there for two or three hours.

Two. Or three.

I don't know what happened inside that shop. Time behaved differently. Perhaps there was a black hole somewhere between the saree racks. I was the only man there, watching two women examine sarees with the sort of attention I usually reserve for a complicated research problem.

And I bought three sarees.

Three.

I am still not entirely sure how that happened. But I did, and I have no regrets. Well, perhaps a few questions.

After that, we bought some street clothes and went to Chitale Bandhu to pick up snacks for home. Then came misal pav and jhal muri, followed by fruit juice. I loved the fruit juice. It was one of those small, entirely ordinary things that somehow makes a tiring day feel worth it.

By the time we returned, I had got to know both of them a little better. We talked about families, and I heard them speak to their mothers and in-laws. The day had become less about sightseeing and more about spending time together.

And somewhere in all this, I had started liking Niyati.

She was very innocent. Like a cow, I thought. I don't know why we call innocent people cows. Cows have probably done nothing to deserve being dragged into our emotional vocabulary, but the comparison made sense to me at the time.

She was sweet and kind in a way that didn't seem deliberate. She wasn't trying to impress anyone. At one point, she gave one of her new earrings to Julius Max, who had lost one in India, while Lena was standing right beside us. It was such a small gesture, but I noticed it.

I noticed quite a few small things about her, actually.

We travelled together in a cab, and I enjoyed her company. I liked listening to her talk. I liked the way she was with people. I was beginning to look forward to the moments when we would all be together, and I was also beginning to notice that I was looking forward to one person in particular.

I did not have a plan for this.

For once, there was no plan.

Usha had to leave on Thursday afternoon. Around the same time, my sister told me that there would be a Garba night on Friday and that she could book passes for me. I asked her to book two.

I will not explain why I asked for two. I think the reader can manage.

Meanwhile, on the bus to the director's dinner on Wednesday, Niyati started inviting people for Garba because passes were available online. I asked someone to make a WhatsApp group and booked tickets for the people who wanted to come. Then more people heard about it, and more people wanted to join. Word of mouth did its thing, another group was created, and somebody else booked tickets for them.

At this point, I was apparently running a small Garba-ticket operation in addition to attending a scientific conference.

Friday arrived. We met at 6 p.m., took photographs, and divided ourselves into three groups so that each group had at least one person who knew the others. We got separated, but that was the arrangement. We reached the venue.

And then I saw Niyati.

The girl who had been feeling weak and tired was suddenly in full-energy mode. It was as if someone had replaced her battery overnight. She was happy, lively, and completely different from the person who had been wondering whether a six-minute walk was too much.

I didn't enjoy dancing all that much. Garba, however, is a different matter. So I played along for a while. On a few occasions, I found myself close to her, putting a hand on her shoulder and such. She noticed.

Of course she noticed. I wasn't exactly being subtle, was I?

After Garba, we went to La Plasir for Italian food. By the time we got back to our rooms, it was quite late. Usha had left that afternoon, which meant Niyati was alone in her room.

Later, she called me.

We talked about our lives. Not the usual surface-level conversation, not just conference gossip or where to eat the next day. She opened up a little. Not completely, but enough for me to feel that something had shifted. Perhaps it was the Garba energy. Perhaps it was the fact that we had spent so much time together. Perhaps it was simply that sometimes people feel comfortable with each other without knowing exactly why.

We talked until 3 or 4 a.m.

I should probably mention that we had a conference closing day to attend the next morning. We were adults, supposedly responsible researchers, and yet there we were, sacrificing sleep for a conversation neither of us seemed keen to end.

I was tired. She was tired. Neither of us was particularly interested in being sensible.

The next day was the closing day of the conference. After that, my sister, some friends, Niyati, and I went to Sinhagad Fort.

We had slept late, and we were all running on very little sleep. Niyati talked about some things from her past that day. We were both tired, but I remember listening carefully. There are moments when you realise that someone is trusting you with a part of themselves they don't usually show everyone. I didn't want to take that lightly.

We had hired a cab to take us to the parking point and bring us back to a particular location. We had breakfast at the starting point. Later, I took Niyati to a juice place and ordered a dry-fruit mango mastani.

I knew it would work.

Well, I didn't *know* know. But I had a feeling.

She loved it.

And I was absurdly pleased with myself. I had managed to choose something she liked. It was a small victory, but I was happy to take it.

On the way back to Prithvi, we shared a cab. She was sleepy, and her shoulder rested against me. Not her head, just her shoulder.

Just her shoulder.

I was on cloud eleven.

For the next forty or fifty minutes, I had absolutely no complaints about traffic, the road, the cab, the driver, or the world. I could have travelled to another state like that. I could have paid the ₹10,000 flight fare all over again. (No, perhaps not. Let's not get carried away.)

We reached Prithvi, rested for a while, and met again to settle some accounts. Niyati wanted to buy gifts for everyone. I helped her place the orders, and she asked me what I wanted.

What could I say?

I wanted someone, not something.

Naturally, I said nothing of the sort.

She had a train to catch at 9 p.m. I offered to drop her at the station. She said there was no need to come if I wasn't going to accept a gift. I had a feeling she wanted me to accompany her, though I wasn't sure. I didn't want to assume too much.

In the lift, I asked, “Should I come?”

“No,” she said.

Then, almost immediately, “Yes.”

And that was that. We went to the station.

I had something I wanted to say. In fact, I had wanted to say it since the previous night. But the moments never quite lined up. There was always someone around, something to do, a train to catch, or a sentence that I couldn't bring myself to finish.

My flight was at 5 a.m. the next morning. I left for the airport, carrying all the things I had brought back from Pune, and something else I hadn't expected to carry.

I reached the airport and kept thinking about the same thing.

Should I tell her? Should I wait? Is this just the exhaustion and excitement of a few days together? Am I reading too much into things? What if I make it awkward? What if I don't say anything and spend the next few weeks wondering what might have happened?

My mind was going in circles.

At 3:33 a.m., I finally sent her a message.

> I am thinking in a loop, it's not right for me to text this at 3 am.
>
> (I would prefer it over a call, ideally tomorrow)
>
> But it's also not right to keep you in suspense (for the whole night)
>
> these few days with you have been really special for me. Somewhere along the way I started feeling more than friendship, and I didn't want to hide that from you. I'm not asking for any answer or promise right now. I just wanted to ask if you'd be open to us exploring this slowly and seeing where it goes. And honestly, whatever you feel, our friendship stays exactly as it is from my side.

Then I waited.

I don't remember how long it took for her reply to arrive. Perhaps it wasn't long at all. Waiting has a strange way of stretching time, especially when the message you are waiting for might change how you look at everything that has happened over the last few days.

Her reply began:

> There are so much to say, at 0333, but I would say few points...
>
> Yes, I would prefer on call also tomorrow aaram se,
>
> Thanks for keeping not suspense...

She said that the whole thing was special for her too. That she had never received this much special treatment, respect, care, and understanding. She didn't know where it would go. Her past experiences made her cautious, and she didn't want to be broken or hurt anyone. But she wanted to be optimistic and open to exploring this, provided it didn't break her.

And then she wrote:

> If you are okay with my turkish love for Flavius, then I am open for this exploring shows as you said.... 😃😂❤️ I am not expecting any right now but just be with me as you are

I read that message, and I smiled.

The Turkish love for Flavius was a reference I wasn't going to ignore, obviously. Even in a moment like this, she managed to make me laugh. And perhaps that was one of the things I liked about her: there was room for seriousness without everything becoming serious all the time.

I had gone to Pune for a conference. I had spent ₹10,000 on a flight because I had forgotten that buses existed. I had sat in an airport all night, met a friendly cab driver, followed two women across a road, bought three sarees, complained about poha, organised a Garba outing, visited Sinhagad Fort, and developed an unreasonable attachment to a forty-minute cab ride.

And somewhere in between all that, I had met someone I wanted to know better.

I still don't know what the most important part of the trip was. The conference was important, of course. That was the reason I went. The poster, the research, the conversations with other researchers — all of it mattered.

But when I think about Pune now, my mind doesn't first go to a conference hall or a poster session.

It goes to a road crossing.

A saree shop that swallowed three hours.

A mango mastani.

A shoulder resting against mine.

And a message sent at 3:33 in the morning, because I couldn't keep my thoughts in a loop any longer.

The train ticket wasn't confirmed. I had panicked. I had booked an expensive flight. None of that was part of the plan.

Meeting her wasn't part of the plan either.

Maybe that is why I remember it all so well.
