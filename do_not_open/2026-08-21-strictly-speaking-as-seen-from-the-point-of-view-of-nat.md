Strictly speaking, as seen from the point of view of nature, it is unnecessary Human beings do not require their food to be ses somed. Salt, sugar, spices, and fla vouring these are all additions we have made to our diet, em bellishments that evolution does not insist on. The taste inherent in food is meant to be good enough, in any case, hunger is an instinct that is far deeper than our need for taste.

And yet, today it is impossible to think of food without the seasoning. While this is true everywhere, it is particularly so in In dia, where blandness in food is anathema. Somewhere along our evolutionary journey, we discovered that our taste buds could be stimulated by adding flavours that created a new experience of food. It is as if we found a specialist that catered exclusively to our taste buds, revelling in creating experiences that made them squeal in delight.

The relationship between food and sea-soning offers an interesting commentary on how humans navigate the boundary between necessity and pleasure. Food without season-ing is perfectly capable of keeping us alive, much like a house without windows could technically provide shelter. And yet, we re-sist this utilitarian reduction of life to its bare essentials. We seem to have a deep-seat-ed need to transform the necessary into the meaningful, to find poetry in the prosaic.

This transformation happens through what might be called the alchemy of en-hancement. Tiny additions-a pinch of salt, a mysterious combination of spices, a dash of herbs change the experience of eating radi-cally So much taste delivered through such few milligrams of substance.

Conceptually, seasoning acts as an in tensifier of food while not really being food itself. Seasoning is edible, of course, but it isn't quite food. It is a magic drizzle of spe cific flavours and tastes. It heightens taste notes as well as adds new ones to turn the substance of food into something nature had not intended it to be. Seasoning is a cheat code for the taste buds, a way to turn some-thing functional into something pleasurable.

Perhaps it is the same instincts that creat ed cave paintings and gave birth to music. The desire to adorn, to underline our presence in a way that went beyond the imperatives of evolution. Things that felt superfluous but be came defining for human beings. Seasoning is to cooking what music is to sound.

This pattern of finding meaning in en-hancement rather than essence plays out in more mundane domains of human experi ence. Think of how we dress up formal com-munication with pleasantries and social nice ties-technically unnecessary for conveying information, but essential for making human interaction palatable. Or consider how we embellish our homes with objects that serve no practical purpose but make the space feel more lived in', more ours. It is interesting that evolution has not deemed it necessary to make food elf self-sufficiently tasty. It is unlike ses, which has co-located with pleasure to ensure that the human race continues. But the other es sentials of life, like food, water and air, hve been left unembellished, presumably because we are hardwired to seek these, Seen this way seasoning's role is to make food sensual. It converts not only food into a form of our art, but eating becomes an act of discernment.

The transformation of the raw to the cooked was an act of culture. The transformation of the cooked to the seasoned is an act of refine ment. It converts the universal into the particu lar, translates substance into style. The Impulse to season food appears to be universal across cultures, but the specific vocabulary of season ing is intensely local. In India, where it is popu larly said that food changes every hundred kilo metres, what changes most is the seasoning.

Seasoning offers a particularly instructive metaphor because of how it operates-not by overwhelming the original but by bring ing out its hidden qualities. Good seasoning doesn't mask the food; it enhances, it reveals it, perhaps slants it in a particular direction. one that is most flattering to it. This suggests something profound about how meaning of ten emerges not through radical transforma tion but through thoughtful enhancement. It imagines change as making a thing more like itself than like something else.

In a world obsessed with efficiency where we try to frim the fat in as many parts of our lives as possible, it is instructive to remember the value that gets added to our lives through the apparently unnecessary How boring and meaningless our lives would be if we ate mere ly for sustenance and wrote merely to be able to send memos with appropriately marked ccs. Seasoning stands as a testament to how hu mans consistently choose to make life more in-teresting than it needs to be, how we find ways to insert meaning and pleasure into the cracks between necessity and survival.

It is clear that for human survival is not enough. We need to make survival interesting Perhaps our brains need their pound of flesh, they do not let the body's needs limit what it means to be human. In some ways, our need for seasoning tells us we need beauty and art in our lives. Not as an indulgence, but as proof that we are human.
