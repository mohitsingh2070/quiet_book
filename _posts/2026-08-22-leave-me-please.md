title: "When a Relationship Becomes Small"
category: "thought"
subtitle: "On caring deeply, letting go, and refusing to live on scraps."
---

Two days ago, I was sitting in a parking lot, staring at my phone for twenty minutes, trying to draft a text message.

It shouldn't have been that hard. I was just responding to someone who used to be one of my favorite people in the world. But over the past six months, everything between us had shifted into this bizarre, careful dance.

The long texts (for some it might be late-night calls) about our deepest secrets had turned into polite, hollow check-ins. The inside jokes were gone, replaced by surface-level updates about work and the weather.

I sat there typing and erasing, typing and erasing.

I was trying to make myself sound casual. I didn't want to seem "too much." I was trying to figure out how to love this person less just so we could keep being friends.

And right there, with my thumb hovering over the screen, I caught my breath and thought:

> *What on earth am I doing?*

The worst part about a dying relationship isn't usually as dramatic as we think.

It's a slow and quiet downgrade.

It's when you notice that you can't be fully honest anymore without making the other person retreat. So, instead of walking away, you try to adjust.

You tell yourself:

*"Okay, we can't talk about deep stuff anymore, but we can still grab coffee once a month."*

Or:

*"We can't be real lovers, but maybe we can be casual."*

You convince yourself that having a piece of someone is better than having nothing at all.

But settling for scraps bleeds you dry.

Every time you hang out with someone and force yourself to keep things light, you leave the room feeling emptier than if you had just stayed home alone.

You aren't actually keeping the friendship alive; you're just dragging out the funeral.

When you're built with a heart like mine — I like to call it a *high-capacity heart* — playing it cool feels like wearing shoes three sizes too small.

You can walk in them for a little bit, but eventually, your feet start to bleed.

I used to look at people who could do casual relationships with envy.

I'd watch friends who could text someone once every six weeks, trade a few words, feel totally fine, and go about their day.

I thought maybe I was missing something.

I thought my inability to do "light and easy" meant I was broken, needy, or intense in a bad way.

It took me a long time to realize that some of us are just wired to go all-in.

When we care about a project, we give it our all.

When we love a friend, we remember their birthday, we know their childhood trauma, and we show up at their door when their world falls apart.

When we're in a relationship, we want the whole truth, not just the highlights.

We don't know how to skim the surface of an ocean when we were built for the deep end.

People get uncomfortable when you draw a hard line.

If you tell someone,

> *"I love you, but I can't be in a relationship with you when you constantly hold back, so I'm stepping away,"*

they often call you dramatic.

They say you're being extreme, or bitter, or that you're holding a grudge.

But it's not bitterness.

It's respecting what you used to have enough to refuse to watch it rot into a polite, fake acquaintance.

It's saying:

> *What we had was real and beautiful. I refuse to turn it into small talk about traffic.*

Cutting someone off completely isn't always an act of anger.

Most of the time, it's an act of peace.

It's accepting that two people can love each other, or mean something to each other, and still reach a point where their capacities no longer match.

Instead of sitting around resenting them for not giving you more, or hating yourself for wanting too much, you just close the book quietly and cleanly.

---

*Some endings don't need an argument.*

*Sometimes you simply realize that the version of the relationship you are trying to preserve no longer exists.*
