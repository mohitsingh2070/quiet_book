<p class="post-intro">
I don't think the Ramayana happened for us only on the stage at SAC Hall.
That was just the last one-and-a-half hours of a story that had been quietly
happening for quite some time.
</p>

<p>It probably started even before the skit.</p>

<p>
When I joined IISc, I came to know that people from VSC were reading the
Valmiki Ramayana every day. So I joined them too, a little late, but somehow
I stayed. Some days there would be only two people sitting there, and on some
days it would be almost house full. We read, discussed, and later even started
doing quizzes as part of outreach, discovering little facts that we had somehow
never noticed before.
</p>

<p>
That itself is a different story. The A Block Ramayana reading circle deserves one.
</p>

<p>But then came the idea of taking this story from the pages to the stage.</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-wide">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/rasas_and_scenes_depicting_them.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Notes and ideas about rasas and scenes for the Ramayana production"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Some of the ideas that slowly turned the reading into a stage production.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
OLU and Sangeerth approached OLU with the idea that, as part of Samskrit Sangha,
we could enact the Ramayana in a drama format. The first thought was that it
should be in Hindi, because that would make it easier to get their backing.
Fair enough.
</p>

<p>
Then we had another idea. Why do it entirely in Hindi? Wherever Rama goes,
why not let the language change with the place? We thought of putting different
parts in different Indian languages. It sounded wonderful when we said it.
</p>

<p><em>It did not happen.</em></p>

<p>So finally, Hindi it was.</p>

<p>
And that immediately created another problem. Many of us were not even native
Hindi speakers. But I have to say this: the Telugu people involved in this were
something else. If I had to describe them in one word, gems. They would just
show up, help, learn, and somehow make things work.
</p>

<p>
Then came the question: who will write the script?
</p>

<p>Sangeerth took it up.</p>

<p>
And when Sangeerth writes something, apparently it cannot just be a script.
It became something very close to a research paper.
</p>

<p>
There were references like, “In Valmiki Ramayana this is there, but in Tulsidas
Ramayana this is not there...” and so on. It was almost a literature review.
I actually liked it because it was informative, but for a drama we felt it was
too academic. We needed people to feel the story, not sit there wondering
whether they were attending a seminar.
</p>

<p>
Someone suggested making it more natural. And from there came the idea of
the rasas.
</p>

<p>
So we thought, why not select all the rasas and build the play around them?
</p>

<p>
This was also around the time OLU gave us another small obstacle.
</p>

<p><strong>One hour.</strong></p>

<p>
Actually, they were saying 45 minutes.
</p>

<p>
We were thinking something closer to, “I don't think this can be done in less
than 24 hours.”
</p>

<p>But anyway, we started drafting.</p>

<p>
I think we wrote four or five versions from scratch. One of them was
practically “Bible Production Shri Ramayana.” Another one tried to bring
the rasas into the story.
</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-medium">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/Scenes_to_be_taken_care_of.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Scenes and production notes for the Ramayana"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>At some point, the script became a collection of things that needed to be fixed.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
Then came our internal critic, Sudarshan.
</p>

<p>
He pointed out something that was difficult to argue with: it looked as though
we were forcing the bhavas into the story instead of letting them naturally
come out of the audience. He was also right about something even more basic.
</p>

<p><strong>There was no continuity.</strong></p>

<p>So, once again, we were back to square one.</p>

<p>
At that point I finally said, okay, I will do it myself. I will make one version.
God knows what that version was going to become.
</p>

<p>But then, naturally, everyone had opinions.</p>

<blockquote>
“This is not okay.”<br>
“That part doesn't work.”<br>
“This should change.”
</blockquote>

<p>
That was the point where I lost it.
</p>

<p>
So I did the most democratic thing possible.
</p>

<p>
I divided every kanda among different people and told them to work on it.
</p>

<p>
And people actually showed up with homework.
</p>

<p>
Aditya and Vishnu, especially, did it sincerely. Trivikrama, or maybe
Sudarshan, depending on whose memory we trust, used GPT for his part, and
the output even had emojis in it. That was funny enough at the time, but
strangely, all of this became useful.
</p>

<p>
From those pieces came what eventually became <strong>draft_v4</strong>.
And draft_v4 stayed with us almost till the very end.
</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-full">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/budget_overflow.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Budget notes and calculations for the production"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>The budget, like everything else, had a tendency to grow beyond the original plan.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
Then came the next question.
</p>

<p><em>Who is going to play whom?</em></p>

<p>
I thought this was going to be difficult. We needed people who not only fit
the role physically, but also had the right posture, expressions and, in some
cases, a certain mental and devotional inclination.
</p>

<p>
We did not have to look very far.
</p>

<p>
The A Block mess was literally where we found some of our answers.
</p>

<p>
I remember seeing a girl there whose gait somehow reminded me of Sita.
I didn't know her. Through friends of friends I got her name, and then,
very subtly, started trying to figure out whether she had the devotional
side needed for the role.
</p>

<p>
Fortunately, Telugu people being Telugu people, someone introduced us at
the common table.
</p>

<p>
And then she invited me to her Kuchipudi performance that very evening.
I think there was some sports-event felicitation programme happening too.
</p>

<p>I went.</p>

<p>And whatever doubt I had disappeared.</p>

<p><strong>I knew she was the one.</strong></p>

<p>Sita was found.</p>

<p>
Then came Rama.
</p>

<p>
Finding someone for Rama is not exactly like finding someone for a random
college skit. Somehow I remembered a guy who I thought would fit the role.
I had seen his sincerity in the English class. His name was Hari Shankar.
</p>

<p>
We had formally met in Sarah Talat's English course, and I had already noticed
him there. I remember thinking, in a completely different context, that I
would have selected him for the Service Selection Board for the Navy.
</p>

<p>Now I was selecting him for Rama.</p>

<p>
I asked Manasa and Venkatesh whether he was the right person.
</p>

<p>They gave me their blessing.</p>

<p>I was 100% sure.</p>

<p>
Interestingly, Manasa and Venkatesh were also the people who helped introduce
me to our Sita through Suswara.
</p>

<p>And she readily agreed.</p>

<p>Then people started joining.</p>

<p>
A lot of that happened because of Sudarshan.
</p>

<p>
Sudarshan is one of those people who somehow makes people gather around him.
People hover around him like bees around a flower. He is just that friendly.
</p>

<p>
Trivikrama chose Hanuman.
</p>

<p>
I was not completely convinced.
</p>

<p>
He was always a little out of control, almost like Bala Hanuman himself,
and I was genuinely worried that he might do something completely unexpected
on stage.
</p>

<p><em>Please remember this as point number one.</em></p>

<p>
Then came Karna Priya as Kaikeyi. Someone had to take a negative role,
and she had the courage to do it.
</p>

<p>
Noorie took Manthara, and that is something we simply could not have done
without her.
</p>

<p>Then Ritvik joined.</p>

<p>
People told me he was from Rangmanch, the drama club of IISc.
</p>

<p>
I did not like him as Sugreev.
</p>

<p>Not because he was bad.</p>

<p>Quite the opposite.</p>

<p>I was jealous.</p>

<p>
I felt he had got the better role because Sugreev had expressions and I
thought, “Why does he get to do all that?”
</p>

<p>
I was probably also fighting some completely unnecessary battle for power
in my head.
</p>

<p><strong>Boy, was I wrong.</strong></p>

<p>
Ritvik ended up acting for the entire crew. He showed us that you can have
expressions even in a scene where you have no dialogue.
</p>

<p>I was still jealous, though.</p>

<p>
Then Sudarshan, who had initially been trying for Sita, finally settled into
Ravana.
</p>

<p>
I didn't like him as Ravana either. The problem was that I knew him personally,
and I simply could not imagine him as Ravana.
</p>

<p>
But we were a small group.
</p>

<p><em>Beggars can't be choosers.</em></p>

<p>So Ravana he became.</p>

<p>And somehow I became Lakshmana.</p>

<p>
I don't know exactly why. Apparently people had seen my anger.
</p>

<p>
I still don't know why everyone thought I was appropriate for Lakshmana
because of that.
</p>

<p>But okay.</p>

<p>Lakshmana it was.</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-wide">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/ravana_on_scooter_with_vishnu_behind_and_chamaras_guy_trivikrama_and_aditya.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Members of the Ramayana production in costume and preparation"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Somewhere along the way, the cast stopped being names on a list and became a team.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
Then we had our meeting with the OLU officers and Chairperson about the venue
and dates.
</p>

<p>
The first date was 19 August.
</p>

<p>
For the venue, Sudarshan and Trivikrama were trying for J. N. Tata Auditorium.
We had a small crew, but the ambition was basically: either succeed or fail
spectacularly.
</p>

<p>
J. N. Tata was a huge auditorium. The funny part was that we were a bunch of
amateurs wondering who would even come to watch us there.
</p>

<p>
Sangeerth and I were thinking about Satish Dhawan Auditorium, but it was under
renovation during that period.
</p>

<p>
Then OLU came up with another solution.
</p>

<blockquote>“You can do it in the Main Building.”</blockquote>

<p>LOL.</p>

<p>
There wasn't even a proper stage. It was basically meant for lectures.
</p>

<p>So we said, no, we'll take SAC.</p>

<p>They agreed.</p>

<p><strong>₹3,000 to book it.</strong></p>

<p>Then came funding.</p>

<p>
Initially, we didn't really know what the cost would be. Sudarshan and
Trivikrama were very clear that they wanted at least one lakh, not less.
</p>

<p>
OLU said it would be ₹50,000 including SAC charges.
</p>

<p>We said okay.</p>

<p>
And then, as budgets usually do in college, every budget allocation started
exceeding ₹50,000.
</p>

<p>
We were worried about the spending as though our salary was less than our
expenses.
</p>

<p>
But we thought, okay, we are middle class. We know how to manage.
</p>

<p>
In between all of this, dialogues were being refined.
</p>

<p>
Surpanakha also started coming for practice.
</p>

<p>
We decided to practice kanda-wise so that people whose roles appeared in a
particular section could come for that practice.
</p>

<p>
And then Sudarshan did something that, looking back, was probably one of the
biggest organisational things in the whole play.
</p>

<p>
He created <strong>27 groups.</strong>
</p>

<p>
Props. Music. Songs. Costume. Background. And then more groups kept appearing
whenever a new requirement appeared.
</p>

<p>
We even had women-role groups because we had fewer women in lead roles, so
the idea was to bring in their friends and get more people involved.
</p>

<p>
The problem was that we had nowhere to practice.
</p>

<p>
So we practiced in the OPB corridor.
</p>

<p><strong>Yes.</strong></p>

<p>A Ramayana drama.</p>

<p>In a corridor.</p>

<p>
One day there was an exam there and we were literally kicked out.
</p>

<p>
That was when we raised the issue with OLU and asked for the CCE Hall.
OLU communicated with CCE Hall, and they agreed.
</p>

<p>Finally, a place.</p>

<p>
Although only for some time because new admissions were going on and they
needed the hall for admission counselling.
</p>

<p>So we practiced there.</p>

<p>
And somehow, despite everything, I still felt we were at square one.
</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-medium">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/townhall_meeting.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Meeting related to the Ramayana production"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>There were meetings, permissions, venues, budgets and a surprising number of things to figure out.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
Then one day Ritvik said he had to go home and might not be able to continue
playing Sugreev.
</p>

<p>I was shocked.</p>

<p>
He was the one person in the cast who felt mature enough to hold the thing
together, and suddenly he was leaving.
</p>

<p>That was a serious eye-opener.</p>

<p>We doubled down on practice.</p>

<p>
And then something else happened that changed the entire game.
</p>

<p>
Using microphones for the voices was going over budget.
</p>

<p>So we thought of using recorded voices.</p>

<p>Kaushik came in.</p>

<p>
And Kaushik, along with Ritvik, helped record the voices, with Debdyuti of
course, because Debdyuti was somehow everywhere.
</p>

<p>
I genuinely don't think I had the larger picture at that point.
But Kaushik somehow did.
</p>

<p>
He had this grand vision of how it could actually look and sound.
</p>

<p>
Once we started practicing with the audio files, things became different.
</p>

<p>
Debdyuti also doubled down on every cast member to come and record their voice.
He even did the director's scolding part rather nicely.
</p>

<p>
Then came Lava and Kusha, our two main sutradharis, Abhiram and Sanjeev.
They were almost like our own people from the daily VSC class.
</p>

<p>And they were extremely sincere.</p>

<p>
At some point we started wondering what we would do during admission time,
but by then we had enough people to keep going.
</p>

<p>
And then, for the first time, we practiced inside SAC.
</p>

<p>The stage was huge.</p>

<p>And damn, it was scary.</p>

<p>
A huge stage with no props looks even bigger. It was just empty space staring
back at us.
</p>

<p>
Then Manasa came in to fill the gap.
</p>

<p>
Mayank and Ravi also joined us, although I didn't personally know either of them.
</p>

<p>
And Vishnu took the props side to another level.
</p>

<p>
I saw them making props on weekends, with so much alacrity, and somehow turning
things that existed only in our heads into things we could actually put on a stage.
</p>

<p>
Then came Chandan ji.
</p>

<p>
I had complete confidence in him.
</p>

<p>
But he was busy with his PhD, had just finished his last year, and had family
and kids to take care of. People started saying we might have to remove his
scene altogether or find a replacement.
</p>

<p>
I think I stood by him.
</p>

<p>
Trivikrama and Sudarshan kept asking me to rope him in for every practice.
He came for a few of them.
</p>

<p>I still felt he would do it flawlessly.</p>

<p>Then suddenly we were at T-15 days.</p>

<p>
And SAC became a problem again.
</p>

<p>
There are many clubs on campus, Rangmanch, Kabaddi, Marathi Mandal and so on,
and the venue had to be divided.
</p>

<p>So we got two days.</p>

<p><strong>Two days.</strong></p>

<p>
Somehow we pushed, negotiated and managed to get enough slots for practice.
And we did our best.
</p>

<p>
Again, without Sudarshan, I don't know what we would have done.
</p>

<p>
He brought clothes, shawls, props, practically whatever he could find at home.
</p>

<p>
We would reach around 8:30, dress up and rehearse.
</p>

<p>
And after so much practice, something strange happened.
</p>

<p>
We stopped needing to memorise the dialogues.
They were just coming.
</p>

<p><strong>Flawlessly.</strong></p>

<p>
And then we got more additions.
</p>

<p>Samarth.</p>
<p>Advait.</p>
<p>Aditya.</p>

<p>
We also got a replacement for Sugreev. Ritvik became Titash.
</p>

<p>I still think the original Sugreev was better.</p>

<p>
Maybe you only understand the value of something after you lose it.
</p>

<p>
Before all of this, we had gone back to OLU asking to increase the budget.
He agreed.
</p>

<p>
Although we kept it slightly secret and told only half the truth because
we didn't want to cross the upper threshold.
</p>

<p>
Then came Saturday.
</p>

<p>
We finally got the costumes, thanks to Vijay and his generosity.
</p>

<p>We did a costume rehearsal.</p>

<p>
And that rehearsal exposed a ridiculous number of problems.
</p>

<p>
Costume changes were taking too long.
We didn't know exactly where people should enter.
Some transitions didn't work.
We had to fix all of that.
</p>

<p>
And meanwhile, we also needed to invite people.
</p>

<p>
The Ramayana was not easy to get through bureaucracy.
Permissions had their own Ramayana.
</p>

<figure class="story-image story-image-wide">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/critic-review.jpeg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Critics session and review of the Ramayana production"
       loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Even after everything seemed ready, we still found things to fix.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>
Then Harish ji came with another problem and solution.
</p>

<p>
We didn't have a proper spotlight available.
</p>

<p>
So he brought a focus torch from the lab.
</p>

<p>
At the time I thought it didn't really work.
</p>

<p>Then I saw the final performance.</p>

<p>It was superb.</p>

<p>
Harish was also one of the people who pushed the hype through the poster and
everything around it.
</p>

<p>
By then, we knew the flow.
We knew where we had to work.
But we still decided to have a critics' session.
</p>

<p>
And that turned out to be the cherry on top.
</p>

<p>
Shankar Rama gave us feedback, some others were there too, but he was
particularly serious about it.
</p>

<p>
And we realised we had very little time for costume changes.
</p>

<p>That is where Kavya came in.</p>

<p>
She was a latecomer, but she helped tremendously with the transitions.
Eventually we were able to pull the changes off in time.
</p>

<p>
At one point I genuinely felt like Iron Man.
Standing there in the Titanic pose.
</p>

<p>
And behind me, Jarvis was changing my armour.
Kavya, Manasa and, I think, Chitambaram.
</p>

<p>That was basically us.</p>

<p>
Then came the final day.
</p>

<p>
And somehow, everything that had been a problem at some point started falling
into place.
</p>

<p>
For the first time we had LED lights.
We had an LED screen.
</p>

<p>
Sai Kantha, or Sri, and Kaustubh were handling the tiny, tiny details of the
slides and their transitions.
</p>

<p>
Debdyuti, who had apparently decided he was going to learn everything in this
production, started learning how to control the lights like a DJ.
</p>

<p>
Kaushik was there with his weapon, the laptop.
</p>

<p>
There were on-the-spot improvisations too.
</p>

<p>
At one point they used HDMI 1 to 2 so that we could make a blank screen and
avoid having unwanted light on the stage.
</p>

<p>
And then there were Tarun and Venkatesh, our Tamil guy and treasurer,
handling the spotlights.
</p>

<p>
They were doing it so well that we never even noticed them.
</p>

<p>
And I think that is the funny thing about a play.
</p>

<p>
When you finally sit in the audience, you see Rama.
You see Sita.
You see Lakshmana.
You see Ravana.
You see Hanuman.
You see the lights, the screen, the costumes, the music.
</p>

<p>You see one story.</p>

<p>
But behind that one story are 27 groups, corridors, rejected scripts,
research papers, GPT with emojis, budgets that refused to stay budgets,
people getting kicked out of practice spaces, costume changes that took
too long, a torch brought from a lab, recorded voices, people making props
on weekends, someone leaving for home, someone arriving at the last minute,
people who barely knew each other becoming a team, and a hundred tiny
decisions that nobody in the audience will ever know happened.
</p>

<p>
Looking back, it feels almost as if every small thing was placed on our path
exactly when we needed it.
</p>

<p>
I don't think we knew the larger picture while we were inside it.
We were mostly solving the problem that was in front of us.
</p>

<p>
And somehow, one problem after another, we reached SAC Hall.
</p>

<p>
I know I have missed many people.
I know I have missed many small details.
Some names and some moments will probably appear somewhere in another story.
</p>

<p>
But perhaps that is also the beauty of it.
</p>

<p class="post-ending">
The final play was only what the audience saw.<br>
The real Ramayana, at least for us, was everything that happened before the curtain opened.
</p>
