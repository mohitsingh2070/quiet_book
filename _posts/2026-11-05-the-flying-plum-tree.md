---
title: "The Flying Plum Tree"
---

The name of the temple was **Dazaifu Tenmangu**.

I didn't know much about the place before going there.

Actually, I knew almost nothing.

I was just walking around, looking at things, probably taking more photographs than necessary. You know how it is when you visit a new place. Every corner looks like it deserves a photograph, even when you have no idea what you are photographing.

But there was this tree inside the temple.

A plum tree.

And somehow, there was a whole story behind it.

More than a thousand years ago, there was a man called **Sugawara no Michizane**.

He was a scholar, a poet, and a politician. And apparently, he was very good at all three.

Which, as it usually happens with people, made some other people uncomfortable.

The Fujiwara clan had him falsely accused, and he was sent away from Kyoto to Dazaifu.

Exiled.

Before leaving Kyoto, he went back to his garden. There was a particular plum tree there that he loved. He had spent years sitting beneath it, writing poetry.

And knowing that he was leaving it behind, he wrote:

> **東風吹かば 匂ひおこせよ 梅の花**  
> **あるじなしとて 春な忘れそ**

A loose translation:

> *When the east wind blows, send your fragrance to me, O plum blossom.*  
> *Even though your master is no longer there, do not forget the spring.*

And then comes the part I really liked.

The story says the tree missed him so much that it couldn't stay behind.

So one night, it uprooted itself and flew.

From Kyoto all the way to Dazaifu.

(I know. A tree flying across Japan sounds like something someone added later because the original story wasn't dramatic enough.)

But apparently, it landed here. Right beside where Michizane was living.

And that is why it came to be known as **Tobiume — The Flying Plum Tree**.

Today, a descendant of that very tree stands in front of the main sanctuary. And apparently, it is still the first plum tree in the region to bloom every spring.

I don't know how much of the story is true.

Maybe none of it.

Maybe all of it.

But standing there, looking at a tree that people have been telling this story about for more than a thousand years, I was thinking about how strange people are.

We leave places.

We leave people.

Sometimes we are forced to leave.

And sometimes, even when we leave, something of us keeps trying to find its way back.

Maybe that is what we call memory.

Or longing.

Or love.

I don't know.

I just know that I stood in front of a plum tree and thought about someone who left Kyoto a thousand years ago, and somehow, his story was still here.

## A little walk before the fortune

The temple itself was lovely, of course. But the approach to it was also a place to slow down a little. There were shops, people walking in both directions, and the usual combination of things that make you want to stop every few metres.

I don't remember what I was looking for on that street. Maybe nothing in particular. Sometimes the best part of travelling is walking without having a very serious plan.

And then I did something that probably deserves its own small paragraph.

I took a fortune card.

At the shrine, you can take an *omikuji* — a fortune slip that tells you how things might go. I took mine, but I didn't open it.

The reason was simple: if it turned out to be bad luck, I was supposed to tie it at the shrine.

So I thought, let me not open it here. I'll deal with the consequences later.

A very scientific approach to fortune-telling.

I carried the unopened card with me and went back to the hostel. Only there, safely away from the shrine and its potential bad news, did I finally open it.

And guess what?

**No. 15 — 大吉 (Daikichi). Great blessing.**

Of course.

I had spent the whole journey protecting myself from bad luck, only to discover that I had apparently been carrying good luck around in my pocket.

## My fortune: No. 15 — 大吉

The overall fortune was quite encouraging. It said that things were looking extremely favourable, especially in terms of reputation, recognition, and work. People might notice me and my efforts.

There was just one warning: don't get carried away. Don't become too excited or overconfident.

Fair enough. Even the fortune card knows me.

Here is what the rest of it said:

| Category | Fortune |
|:--|:--|
| **願望 — Wish** | Unexpected joy may come, but don't become careless. |
| **待人 — Person you're waiting for** | They will come; you will receive news from them. |
| **失物 — Lost item** | It will be found in a low place. |
| **旅行 — Travel** | Favourable. |
| **争事 — Disputes / conflicts** | Time will resolve things. No need to worry. |
| **転居 — Moving house** | Choose the right time. |
| **建築 — Construction** | Favourable. |
| **商業 — Business** | Good for buying; benefit or profit is indicated. |
| **病気 — Illness** | It will be mild; you will get through it without serious trouble. |
| **縁談 — Marriage / relationship proposal** | Things will come together favourably. |
| **出産 — Childbirth** | Later than expected, but an easy delivery. |
| **学問 — Studies / exams** | Study calmly and with composure; good results in exams or admission are indicated. |
| **求職 — Job seeking** | It may happen suddenly; employment could come unexpectedly soon. |

I particularly liked the one about the lost item.

*It will be found in a low place.*

That's wonderfully specific and completely unhelpful. If I lose something now, I suppose I'll have to start checking under tables, beds, and every other low place in Japan.

And the one about the person you're waiting for — *they will come; you will receive news from them* — was also interesting. I am not going to make any grand predictions based on a piece of paper. But I did read it twice.

The card also said travel would be favourable. At least that part had already worked out.

I still had one small problem, though. Since it was **大吉**, there was no need to tie it at the shrine. I could keep it.

So, after all that careful avoidance of bad luck, I had brought the card back to the hostel for absolutely no reason.

Maybe that was the real fortune: a little suspense, a good story, and one less thing to tie at the temple.

I don't know how much of the flying plum tree story is true. And I don't know how much faith one should put in a fortune card.

But I liked both stories.

One was about a tree that refused to forget the person it belonged to. The other was a small piece of paper telling me that good things might be coming, as long as I didn't get too carried away.

I left Dazaifu with photographs, a fortune card, and a story about a tree that travelled a thousand years ago to find someone.

Not a bad day's outing.
