---

title: "Waiting at the Train Door"
category: "story"
subtitle: "Some things survive without us remembering why."
---

When I think of childhood,

I don't remember birthdays first.
(I don't think we ever cared enough to celebrate them.)

I remember our train journeys.

I was usually behind,
dragging the biggest suitcase.
(I had somehow convinced myself that nobody else could carry it.)

My parents would exchange a smile.
I think they found it easier
to let me believe I was helping.

Or maybe...
they just wanted me to feel
a little older than I really was.

Looking back,

I think they were carrying me
far more than I was carrying that suitcase.

I remember the fights over the window seat.

One of my sisters would already be there before I even realised the train had arrived.
The other never really cared where she sat...
until I got the window.

My father would get down at stations with an empty bottle in his hand.
Back then, platforms still had taps.
(The water always tasted a little strange.
We still drank it.)

He would disappear into a crowd of strangers,
and I would quietly begin imagining impossible things.

The train would leave.
He wouldn't hear the whistle.
We'd go home without him.

I never said any of this out loud.

I just kept looking in one direction
until he appeared again,
walking a little faster than before,
the bottle full,
as if nothing had happened.

He always smiled before climbing back in.

I don't know why that smile fixed everything,
but it did.

My mother would ask
why he took so long.

One sister would already be asleep against the window.
The other would still be asking
for something to eat.

And somehow,
without anybody trying,
everything would become ordinary again.

*It's strange what survives.*

I don't remember
what we talked about on those journeys.
I don't remember
where most of those trains were going.

But I still remember
my feet swinging above the floor,
the familiar jingle of the tiffin
hitting against the bag,
and the relief
of watching my father
step back into the compartment.

Now all three of us
have grown into separate lives.

We meet.
We laugh.
We leave again.

Nobody fights for the window anymore.

Every journey ends with,

*"Message me when you reach."*

Maybe that's our version now
of waiting at the train door.
